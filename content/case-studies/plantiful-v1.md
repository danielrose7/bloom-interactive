# Plantiful

## Helping turn an idea into a business

I joined Plantiful pre-launch as one of three founding engineers. There was an initial Figma design, a small number of models, and no automated test suite yet.

My first project was a data-transformation pipeline that converted MUI table filters and quick-search logic into SQL queries. That system eventually powered more than 40 data grids across the application.

From there, I worked across nearly every part of the product—from the inventory systems required for launch to online ordering, reporting, notifications, and AI-assisted workflows.

By the time I left, Plantiful had become a plant-specific ERP used by more than 50 live customers and had reached $1 million in annual recurring revenue.

## Building the launch product

Plant inventory doesn’t behave like ordinary inventory.

Plants grow, die, move, split, change grades, and become different products over time. A seed might eventually become a hanging basket. Native growers may need to preserve information about the original seed and its source watershed. Organic growers need a traceable history from seed to sale.

The launch product focused on this lifecycle:

- Planned inventory
- Started inventory
- Ready-to-sell inventory
- Expired inventory
- Sales orders
- Automatic allocation of available inventory to orders

These states also determined which current and future orders a production item could fulfill.

Shortly before launch, a coworker went on vacation and I took ownership of the live-availability project for the final weekend. After launch, I continued working on the underlying inventory model, including item-specific task completion, item splitting, and regrading workflows for tree growers.

Custom fields allowed organizations to capture information unique to their operation without forcing every customer into an increasingly rigid core model.

## Selected features

### AI order intake

#### Turn almost any incoming order into a reviewable draft

Some nurseries had full-time staff members who spent much of their day keying in orders.

Those orders could arrive as a photograph of a handwritten note, a landscaping company’s homemade spreadsheet, a nursery-supplied order sheet, or a purchase order generated by another system. A single order could contain more than 100 line items.

I built an inbound email system that accepted forwarded orders from authorized users. It converted the source material into a standardized PDF, extracted the order into a consistent JSON schema, and passed the result through an agent-based matching process.

Matching was its own challenge. Different organizations often use different names for the same plant, so the system needed to compare possible products and recommend the most likely match. Customers and shipping information went through a similar process.

Every intake became a draft. A person reviewed the proposed matches before converting it into a quote or confirmed order.

The goal was not to remove people from the decision. It was to remove the time-consuming interpretation and data entry around it.

Customers could forward an email and review the result instead of deciphering handwriting, interpreting an unfamiliar spreadsheet, and manually entering every line. Some organizations no longer needed to backfill data-entry positions when they became vacant. More importantly, the people doing the work carried less background anxiety about the growing stack of orders waiting to be entered.

### AI reports assistant

#### Let users ask the system for the view they need

Plantiful contains a lot of operational data. Before the reports assistant, users needed detailed knowledge of the available columns and filters. They might have to expose several hidden columns and configure multiple controls before reaching the view they wanted.

That created friction for less technical users, new organizations, support conversations, and sales demonstrations.

I proposed and built an AI reports assistant that lets someone describe a view in natural language:

> Show me orders shipping this week.

> Show me plants in greenhouse five.

The assistant translates the request into the appropriate columns, filters, and sorting. When the request is uncertain, it can continue the conversation to clarify what the user means.

It then presents a summary of the proposed configuration. The user reviews that summary and chooses whether to apply it to the current table.

The difficult part was making the application’s existing data model understandable to an agent. I built unit-tested transformation layers between the codebase and the language model, along with routing, retries, and resilient parsing for responses that did not arrive in the ideal shape.

The feature became useful beyond reporting. Sales could shape demonstrations around a prospect’s actual questions, support could help customers reach complex views more quickly, and new organizations could discover what the platform made available without first mastering every table control.

### Online ordering

#### A wholesale ordering system, not an ordinary shopping cart

Before online ordering, every order passed through an employee at the nursery. Formats varied, and the resulting data entry piled up.

I owned the complete online-ordering feature set.

Wholesale ordering introduced requirements that a conventional storefront does not usually have. Nurseries needed customer-specific pricing. Submissions initially needed to become quotes rather than confirmed orders. Later, we added an option to confirm orders immediately while preventing customers from selling into negative inventory.

Some customers placed orders containing as many as 1,500 line items. They might build those orders in bursts over several days, making lost browser state a genuinely heartbreaking failure. I added device storage so long-running orders could survive refreshes and interrupted sessions.

The original table-based ordering interface was later joined by an optional card view, allowing customers to choose the browsing style that fit their catalog.

For Forest Lake Greenhouses, online ordering changed how the team spent its time:

> The majority of our customers started ordering online as soon as we opened it up. Each order used to take about 15 minutes to input—now it comes straight into the system. We’re at about 65 to 70% of customers ordering online today.

That gave the team time for work that had previously been squeezed out, such as double-checking trucks before they left the nursery.

### Lifecycle and production history

#### Inventory that grows, moves, changes, and dies

Plantiful’s value comes in part from being a plant-specific ERP.

A production item can move from planned to started to ready, and eventually to sold or expired. Along the way, it may move between locations, split into multiple groups, change grades, or become a different sellable item.

Those events affect live availability and determine which current or future sales orders the inventory can fulfill.

They also create an auditable production history. Organic-certification processes that previously relied on handwritten or typed notes across large collections of binders can instead use an item’s Plantiful history to follow it from seed to sale, including cycle counts and location changes.

I contributed to several parts of this system, including item-specific task completion, item splitting, and regrading workflows for tree growers.

### Notifications and configurable workflows

I also built Plantiful’s notification system, including in-app notifications and outbound email.

Notification behavior was controlled through user settings and supported no-code-style workflows. Product events could trigger actions such as syncing with an external system or sending invoices and customer balances on a configurable schedule.

This made notifications more than a list of alerts. They became connective tissue between Plantiful’s internal activity, customer communication, and external systems.

## Building the foundation underneath it

Feature work was only part of my role.

I introduced the automated test suite and helped establish the reusable systems required to support a growing product. The filtering pipeline from my first project expanded to more than 40 data grids. Later AI features relied on tested transformation layers that could tolerate imperfect model output without allowing it to flow directly into consequential actions.

Across the product, the recurring principle was the same: automate the tedious interpretation and configuration work while keeping people in control of the final decision.

## From an idea to a business

I joined when Plantiful was still assembling its founding engineering team and had not yet launched.

I left after helping build a connected platform for inventory, production, ordering, reporting, and operational automation—serving more than 50 live customers and generating $1 million in annual recurring revenue.

The features are satisfying. Helping turn the original idea into a real business is the part I’m proudest of.
